Section 2: The Weak Verb Challenge — Overview

Until now, we have mostly dealt with “strong” roots (like k-t-b or š-b-q), where all three consonants boldly assert themselves in every conjugation. However, some of the most common verbs in human language—to say, to be, to know, to give, to come—are “weak”. This means one of their three root consonants is a “weak letter” (Alaph, Waw, or Yodh), which tends to assimilate, drop out entirely, or turn into a vowel depending on the surrounding sounds.

Cross-Linguistic Comparison: Weak Consonants

Weak verbs are the bane of every Semitic language student, but they follow highly predictable phonetic rules.

  • Hebrew: I-Guttural (אָמַר ʾāmar = he said); I-Yod (יָדַע yādaʿ = he knew); Lamed-He/Yod (בָּנָה bānāh = he built).
  • Arabic: Hollow verbs (قَالَ qāla = he said); Defective/Final Weak (رَمَى ramā = he threw).
  • Syriac:
    • I-Alaph: Root ʾ-m-r (to say) → ʾemar (not ʾamar).
    • I-Yodh: Root y-d-ʿ (to know) → īdaʿ (Yodh acts like an i vowel).
    • III-Yodh: Root ḥ-z-y (to see) → ḥzā (the Yodh drops out, leaving an ā vowel).

Section 3: Morphology of Weak Roots (Peal Perfect)

Despite the spelling changes, weak verbs function syntactically exactly like strong verbs: VSO word order, taking suffixes, and forming the spine of clauses.

1. I-Alaph Verbs (First consonant is Alaph)

The most famous is ʾ-m-r (“to say”). In the Peal perfect 3rd masc singular, the vowel shifts to e:

  • ʾemar (he said), rather than ʾamar.
  • ʾemret (I said), rather than ʾamret.

2. I-Yodh Verbs (First consonant is Yodh)

Very common verbs like y-d-ʿ (to know) and y-t-b (to sit). The initial y fuses with the preceding invisible glottal stop to form an ī vowel.

  • y-d-ʿ → īdaʿ (he knew).
  • y-t-b → īteb (he sat). Note: The verb y-h-b (to give) is highly irregular and behaves like a strong verb without an internal vowel: yahb (he gave).

3. III-Yodh Verbs (Third consonant is Yodh)

Verbs ending in Yodh (like ḥ-z-y, b-n-y, ḥ-y-y). The Yodh falls away in the 3rd masc singular, leaving a final ā vowel. In other persons, the Yodh can reappear as heavily vocalized endings.

  • ḥ-z-y (to see) → ḥzā (he saw) [looks like a 2-consonant word].
  • ḥ-z-y + -et (I saw) → ḥzēt (the ay contracts to ē).
  • ḥ-z-y + -ū (They saw) → ḥzaw (the ay-ū contracts to aw).

Section 5: The highly irregular verbs ʾ-t-y and h-w-y

Two of the most common verbs in Syriac are doubly weak and highly irregular.

  1. ʾ-t-y (to come): I-Alaph AND III-Yodh.
    • 3 m.s: ʾetā (he came).
    • 1 c.s: ʾetēt (I came).
    • 3 m.pl: ʾetaw (they came).
  2. h-w-y (to be/become): Used as an auxiliary verb, or to mean “it happened”. III-Yodh.
    • 3 m.s: hwā (he was / it happened).
    • 1 c.s: hwēt (I was).
    • 3 m.pl: hwaw (they were).

Section 7: The Story — Part 7

The Summons

Narrative note: Pay attention to the massive use of weak roots—ʾemar (said), ʾezal (went), īdaʿ (knew), hwā (was/became), ḥzaw (they saw), ʾetā (came).

Sentence 1

ܟܕ ܐܡܪ ܝܘܚܢܢ ܡܠܬܐ ܕܐܠܗܐ، ܐܙܠܘ ܩܫܝܫܐ ܠܘܬ ܡܠܟܐ kad ʾemar Yūḥannan meltā d-Alāhā, ʾezalū qašīšē l-wāt malkā. kad (when) ʾemar (said) Yūḥannan (John) meltā (the-word) d-Alāhā (of-God), ʾezalū (went) qašīšē (the-elders/guards) l-wāt (to-the-presence-of) malkā (the-king). → When Yukhannan said the word of God, the guards went to the king.

Sentence 2

ܐܡܪܝܢ ܠܗ ܝܕܥܝܢ ܚܢܢ ܡܐ ܗܘܐ ܒܬܪܥܐ ʾāmrīn l-eh: yādʿīn-ḥnan mā hwā b-tarʿā! ʾāmrīn (they-[are]-saying) l-eh (to-him): yādʿīn (knowing) -ḥnan (are-we) mā (what) hwā (happened/was) b-tarʿā (in-the-gate)! → They say to him: “We know what happened at the gate!”

Sentence 3

ܚܙܝܢ ܓܒܪܐ ܘܐܡܪ ܡܠܬܐ ܒܝܫܬܐ ḥzayn gabrā, w-ʾemar meltā bīštā. ḥzayn (we-saw) gabrā (a-man), w-ʾemar (and-said) meltā (a-word) bīštā (evil-[f]). → “We saw a man, and he said an evil word.”

Sentence 4

ܗܘܐ ܗܫܐ ܡܠܟܐ ܪܓܝܙ ܛܒ hwā hāšā malkā ragīz ṭāb. hwā (became/was) hāšā (now) malkā (the-king) ragīz (angry) ṭāb (greatly/very). → Now the king was very angry.

Sentence 5

ܐܡ̣ܪ ܡܠܟܐ ܠܩܫܝܫܐ ܐܝܬܝܘܗܝ ܠܗܪܟܐ ʾemar malkā l-qašīšē: ʾaytāw-hy l-harkā! ʾemar (said) malkā (the-king) l-qašīšē (to-the-guards): ʾaytāw-hy (let-them-bring-him [Aphel imperative]) l-harkā (to-here)! → The king said to the guards: “Bring him here!”

Sentence 6

ܘܐܬܐ ܝܘܚܢܢ ܩܕܡ ܡܠܟܐ w-ʾetā Yūḥannan qdām malkā. w-ʾetā (and-came) Yūḥannan (John) qdām (before) malkā (the-king). → And Yukhannan came before the king.

Section 10: Grammatical Summary

WEAK VERB SHIFT SUMMARY

Class Root Shift Rule 3 m.sg Example 1 c.sg Example
I-Alaph ʾ-m-r First vowel becomes e ʾemar (he said) ʾemret (I said)
